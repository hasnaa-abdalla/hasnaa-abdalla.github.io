From the thesis · Strategic Non-Reconstruction

Through the Sniper’s Eye

A house built for light. A nest for killing. A museum of memory. One building on Beirut’s Green Line, and the two ways of looking out of it.

Hasnaa Abdalla · A scroll story of Beit Beirut

Scroll

1924

1924 · Sodeco, Beirut

The Yellow House

The architect Youssef Aftimus finished the Barakat Building for a wealthy family in 1924, in soft yellow sandstone. Its main idea was openness: an open plan and tiers of arched, columned balconies that let the street and the rooms see each other.

It was built to be looked through. Below it, the intersection carried buses, cars and people about their day.

The building and its intersection, c. 1960.

“I looked at the building and I could see the sky through the building, as if there was nothing behind it. But the light in this particular building was much stronger than the destruction.”

Mona El Hallak, on returning to the intersection after eight years away

The central void, open to the sky.

1975 – 1990 · The Green Line

The Building of Death

When the Civil War split the city into East and West, the house stood directly on the dividing line. The openness Aftimus designed for civic life proved ideal for snipers, who used the building as a nest for fifteen years.

The war killed an estimated 150,000 people. By 1990 the Yellow House had a new name, the Building of Death, and was scheduled for demolition.

The same transparency that let light in let the gun see out.

“Once inside there was an architecture of war, architecture of death. When I first stood behind those walls it was a very cold feeling because suddenly this beautiful building filled with light and yellow stone transformed into a place of death.”

Mona El Hallak, 2012

“It became a bunker where somebody stood and killed people. So I was in the place where the sniper was and I was looking through his eye to the city instead of looking through the architect’s eye to the city.”

Mona El Hallak, 2012

The same opening

Two ways of looking at one city

Stand where Hallak stood. Choose whose eye you look through.

Through the architect’s eye, the opening frames a neighbourhood: a bus, a passer-by, a street that belongs to everyone. Through the sniper’s eye, the same opening is narrowed to a slit, and the same street becomes a field of fire.

This is why Hallak fought to keep the damage. The building does not simply document the war. It makes the visitor stand in both places at once, the architect’s and the sniper’s, so that the before and the during of the war become one space. One cannot understand what happened in this city, the thesis argues, without standing where the killing was done and looking through the killer’s eye.

1994

1994 – 2017 · Twenty-three years

Keeping the wound

Mona El Hallak, then an architecture student, entered the building in 1994 and began a campaign to save it, largely alone, against the institutions that had already demolished downtown Beirut.

Her vision was unpopular: restore it only as far as it needs “to become solid and weatherproof,” and keep the scars.

“The scars were left without beautification so that the wounds remain as witnesses and preservers of memory.”

Al-Jumhuriya, 2016

The ground-floor studio

Faces in the dark

In the photography studio on the ground floor, Hallak found negatives: portraits of Beirut families, mostly from the 1950s to the 1970s. Years later the Photo Mario project cleaned and returned them.

Looking at her father’s portrait, Noura El Turk said it was “like looking at my father’s past through my own eyes.”

“The building was badly damaged, yet as an architect I was intrigued by the void in the middle.”

Mona El Hallak, 2016

The void was designed for civic life. The war made it a killing geometry. Hallak refused to close it.

2017 · Beit Beirut

The museum

The municipality expropriated the building, and an $18 million renovation, partly funded by the City of Paris, reopened it as Beit Beirut. The architect Youssef Haidar added prosthetic concrete that Hallak called “fake bullet holes on the façade” that “ridicule the real ones.”

Still, she said, the building is “strong enough to deny even the additions and to reject them visually.”

Then the doors stayed mostly closed. No permanent director, no collection policy, no programme. By 2017, The Economist reported, “only private events have taken place there,” among them a lavish, invitation-only party for politicians, diplomats and celebrities.

The building is preserved. The amnesia it was built to resist is reproduced inside it.

§ The void kept open

“Not one apology has been made in twenty-two years,” Hallak said in 2012. The war was left out of school curriculums. No national memorials were built. The amnesty law of 1991 gave the militia leaders immunity. And still she chose: “This museum will not ask those questions publicly. No names. No parties. No militias.”

The thesis reads this as a deliberate trade, healing pursued where justice had been shut out. The questions are not asked inside the building. They are built into it. The hole where walls should be, the sky showing through, the narrow slit where a man once waited: the Yellow House keeps open the thing the political system has closed.

The light in this building was stronger than the destruction.
